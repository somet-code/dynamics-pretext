    Furthermore, for synoptic scale motion in the middle latitudes, constants that appear in the equations of motion scale according to Table 8.2.2 below.
    🔗
    Note that the characteristic horizontal length scale \(L\) is 100 times larger than the characteristic vertical depth scale \(H\text{.}\) This is a stand-out feature of synoptic scale weather phenomena: These phenomena are very large horizontally but are confined to the troposphere vertically. As we will see below, these scales lead to interesting simplifications of the equations of motion.
    🔗
    Application of these characteristic scales to the horizontal equations of motion (i.e., the zonal \(x\) and meridional \(y\) component equations of motion) is shown in Table 8.2.3 below, and application of these characteristic scales to the vertical equation of motion (i.e., the vertical \(z\) component equation of motion) is shown in Table 8.2.4 below.
    🔗
    As discussed in the next sub-sections, scale analysis reveals that, to a first-order approximation, mid-latitude synoptic scale motions are in geostrophic and hydrostatic balance.
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.1 Geostrophic balance

    As shown in Table 8.2.3, the two largest terms in each of the horizontal equations of motion are the Coriolis acceleration term proportional to \(\sin{\phi}\) and the pressure gradient acceleration term, each of \(\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})\text{.}\) All other terms are at least one order of magnitude smaller (i.e., at least 10 times smaller).
    🔗
    Neglecting all but these two terms in each equation produces the following simpler system:
    🔗
    \begin{align} 0 \amp = fv - \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\tag{8.2.1}\\ 0 \amp = -fu - \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial p}{\partial y} \tag{8.2.2} \end{align}
    🔗
    (8.2.1) and (8.2.2) represent a balance between the horizontal Coriolis force and the horizontal pressure gradient force. This balance is called geostrophic balance, geostrophic equilibrium, or geostrophy, and the corresponding horizontal wind produced by this balance is called the geostrophic wind. The geostrophic wind is given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{v}_g = u_g \, \hat{i} + v_g \, \hat{j}\tag{8.2.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    so we can write (8.2.1) and (8.2.2) as
    🔗
    \begin{align} 0 \amp = f v_g - \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\tag{8.2.4}\\ 0 \amp = -f u_g - \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial p}{\partial y} \tag{8.2.5} \end{align}
    🔗
    respectively, each of which can be rearranged to give the geostrophic wind components
    🔗
    \begin{align} v_g \amp = \frac{1}{\rho f} \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\tag{8.2.6}\\ u_g \amp = -\frac{1}{\rho f} \frac{\partial p}{\partial y}\tag{8.2.7} \end{align}
    🔗
    (8.2.6) and (8.2.7) show that the meridional (zonal) component of the geostrophic wind depends on the zonal (meridional) component of the horizontal pressure gradient. This dependency can be written compactly in vector form as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \vec{v}_g = \frac{1}{\rho f} \, \hat{k} \times \nabla p\tag{8.2.8} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Given this definition, the geostrophic wind is a horizontal wind that flows locally perpendicular to the pressure gradient and, consequently, locally parallel to surrounding isobars. Since the air parcel horizontal acceleration terms \(\dfrac{Du}{Dt}\) and \(\dfrac{Dv}{Dt}\) are neglected in geostrophic balance, the geostrophic wind is non-accelerating and, therefore, flows in a straight line at constant speed. This produces a diagnostic (descriptive) relationship for the wind, not a prognostic (predictive) relationship.
    🔗
    And the geostrophic wind is only valid for inviscid flow along a flat surface.
    🔗
    Clearly, the above constraints do not apply perfectly to motions in Earth’s atmosphere: Real winds accelerate by changing speed and direction and have vertical components; air is viscous; and winds flow along and parallel to Earth’s curved surface. Furthermore, \(\vec{v}_g\)’s inverse proportionality to the Coriolis parameter means that the geostrophic wind cannot apply at the Equator, where \(f = 0\text{,}\) and the geostrophic wind magnitude greatly overestimates the magnitude of the real wind at tropical latitudes where \(f\) is very small.
    🔗
    But as [Martin (2006)] and [Holton and Hakim (2013)] point out, observed mid-latitude synoptic scale horizontal motions typically are within 10–15% of the geostrophic wind. Thus, geostrophic balance is a useful concept to apply to mid-latitude synoptic scale horizontal motions, and it is worth determining how close to geostrophic real horizontal winds are so we know when and where to safely apply this approximation. As we will see in the next subsection, the Rossby number allows us to do this.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.2 Rossby number

    Since geostrophic balance neglects air parcel horizontal acceleration, it can be useful to determine if air parcel horizontal acceleration for various types of atmospheric motions is "small enough" so that geostrophic balance can apply with sufficient accuracy, or if it is "too large" to meaningfully apply geostrophic balance.
    🔗
    The Rossby number \(Ro\) expresses the ratio of the magnitude of an object’s horizontal acceleration to the horizontal Coriolis acceleration. In other words, it expresses how strongly Earth’s rotation affects an object’s motion.
    🔗
    Table 8.2.3 shows that an air parcel’s horizontal acceleration scales as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{U}{T} = \dfrac{U}{\sfrac{L}{U}} = \dfrac{U^2}{L}\tag{8.2.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and the horizontal Coriolis acceleration scales as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} f_0 U\tag{8.2.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The ratio of the scaled horizontal acceleration to the scaled horizontal Coriolis acceleration gives \(Ro\text{,}\) as shown in (8.2.11) below.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} Ro = \frac{\sfrac{U^2}{L}}{f_0U} = \frac{U}{f_0L}\tag{8.2.11} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Note that (8.2.11) leads to an alternative definition of \(Ro\) as the ratio of the timescale of Earth’s rotation \(T_{rot} = \dfrac{1}{f}\) to the timescale of fluid flow \(T_{flow} = \dfrac{L}{U}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} Ro = \frac{T_{rot}}{T_{flow}} = \frac{\sfrac{1}{f}}{\sfrac{L}{U}} = \frac{U}{fL}\tag{8.2.12} \end{equation}
    🔗
    For mid-latitude synoptic scale motion, we expect \(\dfrac{U^2}{L} \sim 10^{-4} \, \text{m} \, \text{s}^{-2}\) and \(f_0 U \sim 10^{-3} \, \text{m} \, \text{s}^{-2}\text{,}\) producing \(Ro = 10^{-1} = 0.1\text{.}\) This is a low value of \(Ro\text{,}\) and so we say "low-\(Ro\) flows" for which \(Ro \ll 1\) approximately are in geostrophic balance. In other words, geostrophic balance reasonably can be used to approximate low-\(Ro\) atmospheric motions like mid-latitude synoptic scale motions.
    🔗
    As you will learn in a later dynamics course, "high-\(Ro\)" flows for which \(Ro \gg 1\) are not close to geostrophic because air parcels experience horizontal accelerations much greater than the horizontal Coriolis acceleration, and the horizontal Coriolis acceleration consequently can be neglected. For "intermediate-\(Ro\)" flows for which \(Ro \approx 1\text{,}\) air parcel horizontal accelerations are of approximately equal magnitude as the horizontal Coriolis acceleration, and so air parcel horizontal acceleration must be incorporated in (8.2.1) and (8.2.2). In this case, geostrophic balance is not quite a reasonable approximation and must be modified to improve its applicability.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.2.3 Hydrostatic balance

    The two largest terms in the vertical equation of motion are the gravity acceleration term and the pressure gradient acceleration term. All other terms are at least two orders of magnitude smaller (i.e., at least 100 times smaller).
    🔗
    Neglecting all but these two terms in the vertical equation of motion produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = -\frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} - g\tag{8.2.13} \end{equation}
    🔗
    which can be rearranged into the familiar formula for hydrostatic balance:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho g\tag{8.2.14} \end{equation}
