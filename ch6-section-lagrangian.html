    Section 6.1 Lagrangian perspective

    As introduced in Chapter 1, a property \(f\) is conserved for an air parcel if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{Df}{Dt} = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If an air parcel does not exchange mass with its environment—a common assumption—its mass \(m\) is conserved and
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{Dm}{Dt} = 0\tag{6.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    We introduced mass conservation for an air parcel in Chapter 3 when rewriting Newton’s \(2^{\text{nd}}\) law to make it more relevant for atmospheric motions. Now we will build on it.
    🔗
    Since \(m = \rho V\text{,}\) where \(\rho\) is the air parcel’s density and \(V\) is its volume, (6.1.1) becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{D(\rho V)}{Dt} = 0\tag{6.1.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Applying the product rule to (6.1.2) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation} V \frac{D \rho}{Dt} + \rho \frac{DV}{Dt} = 0\tag{6.1.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    which shows that, although the air parcel’s mass can be unchanging with time, its density and volume can change with time in proportion to each other.
    🔗
    Dividing (6.1.3) by \(\rho V\) produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{D \rho}{Dt} + \frac{1}{V}\frac{DV}{Dt} = 0\tag{6.1.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Since the air parcel we have been discussing since Chapter 3 is a rectangular prism with length \(\delta x\text{,}\) width \(\delta y\) and height \(\delta z\text{,}\) its volume is \(V = \delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z\text{.}\) We can expand the second term in (6.1.4) by inserting this definition and applying the product rule.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{1}{V} \frac{DV}{Dt} \amp = \frac{1}{\delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z} \frac{D(\delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z)}{Dt}\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\delta x \, \delta y \, \delta z} \left[ \delta y \, \delta z \frac{D \delta x}{Dt} + \delta x \, \delta z \frac{D \delta y}{Dt} + \delta x \, \delta y \frac{D \delta z}{Dt} \right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\delta x} \frac{D \delta x}{Dt} + \frac{1}{\delta y} \frac{D \delta y}{Dt} + \frac{1}{\delta z} \frac{D \delta z}{Dt} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since \(\dfrac{D \delta x}{Dt} = \delta u\text{,}\) \(\dfrac{D \delta y}{Dt} = \delta v\text{,}\) and \(\dfrac{D \delta z}{Dt} = \delta w\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{V} \frac{DV}{Dt} = \frac{\delta u}{\delta x} + \frac{\delta v}{\delta y} + \frac{\delta w}{\delta z} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As \(\delta x \to 0\text{,}\) \(\delta y \to 0\text{,}\) and \(\delta z \to 0\) (which is an acceptable assumption for an infinitesimally small air parcel),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{V} \frac{DV}{Dt} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = \nabla \cdot \vec{u} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, (6.1.1) becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{D \rho}{Dt} + \nabla \cdot \vec{u} = 0\tag{6.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    which often is rearranged to produce the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{D \rho}{Dt} + \rho \nabla \cdot \vec{u} = 0\tag{6.1.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Either of (6.1.5) and (6.1.6) is called the velocity divergence form of mass conservation since \(\nabla \cdot \vec{u}\) is the velocity divergence, i.e., the divergence of the three-dimensional wind.
    🔗
    Keep in mind that mass conservation does not hold if an air parcel exchanges mass with its environment. If, for example, water is added to an air parcel through evaporation or removed from it as precipitation particles form within the air parcel and fall out of it, (6.1.5) and (6.1.6) must be modified to include source or sink terms. In cases like these, we can use \(\sigma\) [\(\text{kg} \, \text{m}^{-3} \, \text{s}^{-1}\)] to represent a combination of any sources and sinks within the air parcel that cause its mass (and therefore its density) to change. For example, (6.1.6) would become:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{D \rho}{Dt} + \rho \nabla \cdot \vec{u} = \sigma\tag{6.1.7} \end{equation}
