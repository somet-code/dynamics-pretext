    Section 7.2 Thermodynamic energy equation

    The thermodynamic energy equation (also called the thermal energy equation) is produced by dividing the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics for a perfectly dry air parcel given by (7.1.27) by an infinitesimal time interval \(\delta t\) and letting \(\delta t \to 0\text{.}\) The resulting equation is given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \dot{Q} = c_v \frac{DT}{Dt} + p \frac{D \alpha}{Dt}\tag{7.2.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\dot{Q}\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{s}^{-1}\)] is the diabatic heating rate per unit mass, which encompasses the rate at which an air parcel warms or cools due to radiation, conduction, latent heating, and/or frictional dissipation. Note that \(J\) (as in [Holton and Hakim (2013)]) often is used in place of \(\dot{Q}\text{.}\) We will adopt \(\dot{Q}\) going forward, consistent with [Martin (2006)].
    🔗
    The thermodynamic energy equation (7.2.1) says that the rate at which heat is added diabatically to a perfectly dry air parcel from its environment is proportional to the sum of the rate at which the air parcel’s internal energy increases as it warms and the rate at which it does work on its environment by expanding.
    🔗
    Performing the same steps on the related form of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics given by (7.1.28) produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \dot{Q} = c_p \frac{DT}{Dt} - \alpha \frac{Dp}{Dt}\tag{7.2.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The thermodynamic energy equation also can be written in terms of potential temperature. Differentiating the formula for the potential temperature of a perfectly dry air parcel given by (7.1.37) with respect to \(t\text{,}\) rearranging, and substituting the ideal gas law (7.1.5) produce
    🔗
    \begin{equation} c_p \frac{DT}{Dt} - \alpha \frac{Dp}{Dt} = c_p \frac{T}{\theta} \frac{D \theta}{Dt}\tag{7.2.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Substituting (7.2.3) into (7.2.2) and rearranging produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\dot{Q}}{T} = \frac{c_p}{\theta} \frac{D \theta}{Dt}\tag{7.2.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Furthermore, substituting the differential of specific entropy (7.1.29) into (7.2.4) produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{Ds}{Dt} = \frac{c_p}{\theta} \frac{D \theta}{Dt}\tag{7.2.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    which leads to the relationship between specific entropy \(s\) and potential temperature \(\theta\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} s = c_p \ln{\theta}\tag{7.2.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    when integrated over time. Thus, surfaces of constant potential temperature also are surfaces of constant specific entropy and so are called isentropic surfaces.
    🔗
    For an adiabatic process, (7.2.4) tells us \(\dfrac{D \theta}{Dt} = 0\text{,}\) which means \(\theta\) is conserved for a perfectly dry air parcel undergoing an adiabatic process. It further follows from (7.2.5) that \(\dfrac{Ds}{Dt} = 0\) in this case, so \(s\) also is conserved. Thus, a reversible dry adiabatic process is called an isentropic process.
