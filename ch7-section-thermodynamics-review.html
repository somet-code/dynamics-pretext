    🔗
    Additionally, much of what is discussed in this chapter is review from previous meteorology courses, especially courses that include or are devoted to thermodynamics. We will not discuss all concepts in this chapter in class since many are review. Please read through this chapter fully and, if anything seems unfamiliar or fuzzy, cross-reference with [Petty (2008)] and your previous course notes.

    Subsection 7.1.1 Ideal gases and the ideal gas law

    An ideal gas consists of point particles that move randomly and continuously but do not interact with each other except possibly through perfectly elastic collisions. The condition of non-interaction means ideal gas particles are not attracted to each other, do not bond together, and do not undergo phase change. In contrast, the particles of a real gas have size and interact with each other via attraction, bonding, and phase change.
    🔗
    Any gas—ideal or real—can be described by an equation of state that relates its pressure, temperature, and volume. An ideal gas has a simplified equation of state called the ideal gas law. The ideal gas law comes from a combination of Avogadro’s law, Boyle’s law, and Charles’s law and traditionally is written
    🔗
    \begin{equation} pV = nR^*T\tag{7.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(p\) [Pa] is the pressure of the ideal gas, \(V\) [\(\text{m}^3\)] is its volume, \(n\) [mol] is the amount of substance in the gas, \(T\) [K] is the gas’s temperature, and \(R^* = 8.314 \, \text{J} \, \text{K}^{-1} \, \text{mol}^{-1}\) is the universal gas constant.
    🔗
    For many reasons, air is not an ideal gas. Its particles have spatial extent. Organized motion often is imposed on them by external forces, resulting in wind. Some of its particles are attracted to each other and to other substances; arguably most importantly for meteorologists, water molecules regularly experience strong intermolecular attractions as well as attractions to surfaces that result in bonding and phase change.
    🔗
    Luckily, since air molecules are small and attractions usually are weak, air can be treated as an ideal gas with great accuracy under typical atmospheric conditions. Furthermore, we easily can modify its thermodynamic equations to account for the presence of water vapor, such as by replacing temperature with virtual temperature.
    🔗
    Meteorologists typically express the ideal gas law for air as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p = \rho R T\tag{7.1.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p \alpha = R T\tag{7.1.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\rho\) [\(\text{kg} \, \text{m}^{-3}\)] is the air density, \(\alpha = \dfrac{1}{\rho}\) [\(\text{m}^{3} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is its specific volume, and \(R\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\)] is a gas constant appropriate to the particular gas mixture making up the air.
    🔗
    For perfectly dry air (air with no water vapor), we write (7.1.2) and (7.1.3) as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p = \rho R_d T\tag{7.1.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p \alpha = R_d T\tag{7.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    respectively, where \(R_d\) is the gas constant for dry air. Under standard conditions, \(R_d = 287 \, \text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\text{;}\) this value often is used for perfectly dry air.
    🔗
    Although as briefly discussed above we can modify the ideal gas law to apply to pure water vapor and humid (moist) air, for simplicity, the following discussions will refer to perfectly dry air. Please refer to your notes from other courses—particularly your thermodynamics course—and [Petty (2008)] for discussions about applying the ideal gas law to pure water vapor and humid air.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.2 Hydrostatic balance

    For an air parcel in hydrostatic balance (also called hydrostatic equilibrium), the upward-directed vertical pressure gradient force balances the downward-directed gravity force (Figure 7.1.1), and other forces can be neglected. Since the air parcel does not accelerate, the vertical equation of motion ((5.4.3) of Chapter 5) reduces to
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} - g\tag{7.1.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    This balance typically is rearranged
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho g\tag{7.1.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where the negative sign indicates pressure decreases with increasing height in the atmosphere.
    🔗
    When the equation of hydrostatic balance (7.1.7) is separated and integrated, it reveals that the pressure measured at some geometric height is the weight of the atmospheric column above a unit area of measurement at that height.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} - \partial p \amp = \rho g \, \partial z\\ - \int_{p(z)}^{p(\infty)} \partial p \amp = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\\ \int_{p(\infty)}^{p(z)} \partial p \amp = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\\ p(z) - p(\infty) \amp = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since \(p(\infty) = 0\text{,}\) this produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p(z) = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\tag{7.1.8} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\displaystyle \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\) is the weight of a column of air with horizontal cross-section of unit area above geometric height \(z\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Likewise, separation and integration of the equation of hydrostatic balance (7.1.7) between geometric heights \(z_1\) and \(z_2\text{,}\) where \(z_1 \lt z_2\text{,}\) reveals the difference in pressure measured at two geometric heights is the weight of the atmospheric column with horizontal cross-section of unit area contained between those heights:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p(z_1) - p(z_2) = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \rho g \, \partial z\tag{7.1.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(p(z_1) > p(z_2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The equation of hydrostatic balance (7.1.7) also could be written
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial z}\tag{7.1.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    or even
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial p} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\tag{7.1.11} \end{equation}
    🔗
    using the definition of geopotential \(\Phi\) presented in Section 4.8.
    🔗
    Note:
    🔗
    1. The equation of hydrostatic balance (7.1.7) is diagnostic (descriptive) rather than prognostic (predictive) like the vertical equation of motion (5.4.3) because it has no dependence on time \(t\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Vertical motion \(w\) can no longer be solved for using the equation of hydrostatic balance (7.1.7) since \(w\) does not appear in the equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Hydrostatic balance would apply perfectly to the atmosphere if the air was at rest. When we apply hydrostatic balance to a fluid in motion, we call it the hydrostatic approximation (emphasis on approximation) since it is no longer perfectly accurate. But under most atmospheric conditions, excepting those with very large vertical accelerations, the hydrostatic approximation is close to realistic. Consequently, we often approximate atmospheric pressure as the weight per unit area of the atmosphere above the height at which pressure is measured.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.3 Hypsometric equation

    We return to hydrostatic balance as expressed by (7.1.7) and rearrange the ideal gas law for perfectly dry air (7.1.4) to replace \(\rho\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\frac{pg}{R_dT}\tag{7.1.12} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (7.1.12) is a separable equation. We separate its variables
    🔗
    \begin{equation} -\frac{R_dT}{g} \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \partial z\tag{7.1.13} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and integrate it
    🔗
    \begin{equation} - \int_{p_1}^{p_2} \frac{R_dT}{g} \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \, \partial z\tag{7.1.14} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(z_1 \lt z_2\text{.}\) Since pressure decreases with increasing height according to (7.1.7) (and this is observed in nature), \(p_1 \gt p_2\text{,}\) and consequently, it can be helpful to reverse the order of integration on the left-hand side of (7.1.14), which also eliminates the negative sign.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \int_{p_2}^{p_1} \frac{R_dT}{g} \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \, \partial z\tag{7.1.15} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (Note that many texts, including [Martin (2006)] and [Holton and Hakim (2013)], write \(\dfrac{1}{p} \, \partial p\) as \(\partial \ln{p}\text{.}\) This is valid alternative notation.)
    🔗
    If \(R_d\text{,}\) \(T\text{,}\) and \(g\) are held constant, we can integrate (7.1.15) easily. While holding \(R_d\) and \(g\) constant is reasonable in the troposphere since they vary little within it, \(T\) decreases with increasing height linearly on average in the troposphere and cools by over \(60 \, \text{K}\) (as will be discussed in Subsection 7.1.6 below). To avoid writing \(T\) as a linear function of \(z\text{,}\) which complicates (but does not prevent) integration, we typically set \(T = \bar{T}\) in (7.1.15), where
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{T} = \frac {\int_{p_2}^{p_1} T \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p} {\int_{p_2}^{p_1} \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p}\tag{7.1.16} \end{equation}
    🔗
    is the pressure-weighted column-average temperature. Serendipitously, this assumption does not introduce much error! Then, integrating (7.1.15) produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{R_d \bar{T}}{g} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)} = z_2 - z_1\tag{7.1.17} \end{equation}
    🔗
    By defining geometric height thickness (usually simply called thickness) as the vertical depth between two pressure levels,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta z = z_2 - z_1\tag{7.1.18} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(z_1\) is the geometric height of pressure level \(p_1\) and \(z_2\) is the geometric height of pressure level \(p_2\text{,}\) (7.1.17) produces the hypsometric equation.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta z = \frac{R_d \bar{T}}{g} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)}\tag{7.1.19} \end{equation}
    🔗
    According to (7.1.19), the warmer the column-average temperature \(\bar{T}\) between pressure levels \(p_1\) and \(p_2\text{,}\) the greater the thickness \(\Delta z\) of the column of air bounded between those levels. Physically, this is because the faster-moving molecules of warmer air travel greater distances in any given amount of time and expand the column of air. (This also lowers the density of warmer air relative to otherwise-identical colder air.)
    🔗
    Returning to (7.1.12), we can separate it somewhat differently:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} -R_dT \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = g \, \partial z\tag{7.1.20} \end{equation}
    🔗
    In this case, the right-hand side is equivalent to \(\partial \Phi\text{.}\) Then we can integrate
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \int_{p_2}^{p_1} R_dT \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \int_{\Phi_1}^{\Phi_2} \partial \Phi\tag{7.1.21} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (with the order of integration reversed on the left-hand side as before) and apply the pressure-weighted column-average temperature as before to produce
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta \Phi = R_d \bar{T} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)}\tag{7.1.22} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta \Phi = \Phi_2 - \Phi_1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is geopotential thickness, with \(\Phi_1\) as the geopotential at pressure level \(p_1\text{,}\) \(\Phi_2\) as the geopotential at pressure level \(p_2\text{,}\) and \(\Phi_1 \lt \Phi_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Dividing (7.1.22) by \(g_0\) and inserting the definition of geopotential height
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Z = \frac{\Phi}{g_0} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    further produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta Z = \frac{R_d \bar{T}}{g_0} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)}\tag{7.1.23} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The interpretation of (7.1.23) is essentially the same as the interpretation of (7.1.19): According to (7.1.23), the warmer the column-average temperature \(\bar{T}\) between pressure levels \(p_1\) and \(p_2\text{,}\) the greater the geopotential height thickness
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta Z = Z_2 - Z_1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    of the column of air bounded between those levels. As expected, \(Z_1\) is the geopotential height of pressure level \(p_1\text{,}\) \(Z_2\) is the geopotential height of pressure level \(p_2\text{,}\) and \(Z_1 \lt Z_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Figure 7.1.2 below visualizes how a warmer column of air has greater (geometric height, geopotential, and geopotential height) thickness than a colder column of air bounded by the same pressure levels.

    Subsection 7.1.4 \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics

    An air parcel often is conceptualized as a closed thermodynamic system, which is a thermodynamic system that does not exchange mass with its environment but can exchange energy with it through heat and work.
    🔗
    The \(\boldsymbol{1^{\text{st}}}\) law of thermodynamics is a statement of energy conservation for a closed thermodynamic system. It dictates that heat added to a closed thermodynamic system from its environment through absorption results in an increase in the system’s internal (stored) energy and/or causes the system to do work on its environment. This verbal statement is expressed mathematically as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta q = du + \delta w\tag{7.1.24} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(q\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is heat per unit mass (i.e., specific heat), \(u\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is internal energy per unit mass (i.e, specific internal energy), and \(w\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is mechanical work per unit mass (i.e., specific work). (7.1.24) is the form of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics presented in [Petty (2008)]. Further presentations of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law specific to the atmosphere also are consistent with Petty (2008), and the reader should revisit that text for discussion of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics and related topics.
    🔗
    Note that both the inexact differential \(\delta f\) and the exact differential \(df\) are used in (7.1.24) to represent infinitesimal changes. These notations are used in thermodynamics to remind readers that macroscopic (integral) changes in process variables like \(q\) and \(w\) are path dependent, while macroscopic changes in state variables like \(u\) are path independent, respectively. In the context of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics, this means \(\Delta u = u_2 - u_1\) but \(\Delta q \neq q_2 - q_1\) and \(\Delta w \neq w_2 - w_1\text{.}\) Revisit [Petty (2008)] for discussion of state and process variables and exact and inexact differentials.
    🔗
    Also note that work is positive (negative) when it is done by (on) the closed thermodynamic system on (by) its environment.
    🔗
    If a gas does only pressure-volume work on its environment,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta w = p \, d\alpha\tag{7.1.25} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(d\alpha \gt 0\) and consequently \(\delta w \gt 0\) for expansion and \(d\alpha \lt 0\) and consequently \(\delta w \lt 0\) for contraction.
    🔗
    Furthermore, according to Joule’s \(\boldsymbol{2^{\text{nd}}}\) law, the internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on its temperature since its particles are not attracted to each other, and so
    🔗
    \begin{equation} du \propto dT\tag{7.1.26} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Applying these assumptions to (7.1.24) for a perfectly dry air parcel produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta q = c_v \, dT + p \, d\alpha\tag{7.1.27} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(c_v\) is the specific heat capacity of dry air at constant volume. Under stand conditions, \(c_v = 717 \, \text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\text{;}\) this value often is used for perfectly dry air.
    🔗
    Substitution of the differential of the ideal gas law (7.1.3) into (7.1.27) and subsequent rearrangement produce the related form
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta q = c_p \, dT - \alpha \, dp\tag{7.1.28} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(c_p = c_v + R_d\) is the specific heat capacity of dry air at constant pressure. \(c_p \gt c_v\) since heat added to an air parcel during an isobaric process causes the air parcel to expand as well as warm, and so less energy is available for warming. Under standard conditions, \(c_p = 1004 \, \text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\text{;}\) this value often is used for perfectly dry air.
    🔗
    Using the ideal gas law and the definition
    🔗
    \begin{equation} ds = \frac{\delta q}{T}\tag{7.1.29} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(s\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is the entropy per unit mass (i.e., specific entropy) for a reversible thermodynamic process, we can write (7.1.27) and (7.1.28) as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} ds = \frac{c_v}{T} \, dT + \frac{R_d}{\alpha} \, d\alpha\tag{7.1.30} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation} ds = \frac{c_p}{T} \, dT - \frac{R_d}{p} \, dp\tag{7.1.31} \end{equation}
    🔗
    respectively.
    🔗
    As we can see, there are many ways to modify the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics, and the most useful form can be chosen for a given application.

    Subsection 7.1.5 Lapse rates and adiabatic processes

    A temperature lapse rate is the rate of temperature decrease with increasing height. In other words, it is the rate at which temperature cools with increasing height. By this definition, a lapse rate is positive (negative) when temperature cools (warms) with increasing height.
    🔗
    For a location at geometric height \(z\) within Earth’s atmosphere, the environmental lapse rate is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Gamma = -\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\tag{7.1.32} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (Note: Some sources use \(\gamma\) rather than \(\Gamma\text{.}\))
    🔗
    The environmental lapse rate can be used to characterize special atmospheric layers by their temperature behavior in the vertical.
    🔗
    • A temperature inversion is a layer of the atmosphere in which temperature warms with increasing height, and so \(\Gamma \lt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • An isothermal layer is a layer of the atmosphere in which temperature is constant with height, and so \(\Gamma = 0 \, \text{K} \, \text{m}^{-1}\) (or any other appropriate unit).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    An adiabatic process is one in which a closed thermodynamic system does not exchange energy with its environment via heat (although it still can exchange energy with its environment via work). Thus, \(\delta q = 0\) for an adiabatic process.
    🔗
    We apply the adiabatic assumption to the related form of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics (7.1.28), divide by \(\delta z\text{,}\) and let \(\delta z \to 0\) to produce
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = c_p \frac{dT}{dz} - \alpha \frac{dp}{dz}\tag{7.1.33} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (The ordinary derivative can be used if we assume the air parcel experiences changes in temperature and pressure due only to vertical movement; otherwise, a partial derivative can be used.)
    🔗
    Since \(\alpha = \dfrac{1}{\rho}\text{,}\) the second term in (7.1.33) can be replaced with \(g\) by assuming the air parcel is in hydrostatic balance:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = c_p \frac{dT}{dz} + g\tag{7.1.34} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Rearranging (7.1.34) to solve for \(-\dfrac{dT}{dz}\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Gamma_d = -\frac{dT}{dz} = \frac{g}{c_p}\tag{7.1.35} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\Gamma_d\) [\(\text{K} \, \text{m}^{-1}\)] is the dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR). The DALR gives the rate at which a perfectly dry air parcel cools with increasing height as it rises adiabatically while remaining in hydrostatic balance. (Likewise, it gives the rate at which a perfectly dry air parcel warms with decreasing height as it sinks adiabatically while remaining in hydrostatic balance.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.6 Standard atmosphere

    The American Meteorological Society (AMS) defines a standard atmosphere as a "hypothetical vertical distribution of atmospheric temperature, pressure and density that, by international agreement, is taken to be representative of the atmosphere." A standard atmosphere is subject to the assumptions that the air can be treated as an ideal gas and is in hydrostatic balance (at least at lower altitudes). Some standard atmospheres—including the U.S. Standard Atmosphere—are further subject to the assumptions that the air is dry and homogeneously mixed (at least at lower altitudes) and the atmosphere rotates along with Earth, is horizontally homogeneous, is calm, and is a representative average over the diurnal and seasonal cycles.
    🔗
    Several common—and nearly identical—standard atmospheres apply to locations in the middle latitudes, including the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) International Standard Atmosphere (ISA); the U.S. Standard Atmosphere; and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Standard Atmosphere. The U.S. Standard Atmosphere extends through the largest depth (\(z_{min} = -5 \, \text{km}\text{,}\) \(z_{max} = 1000 \, \text{km}\)) and is largely consistent with other commonly used standard atmospheres, and therefore it is chosen for brief presentation here.
    🔗
    The lower portion (through \(z = 130 \, \text{km}\)) of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere’s vertical temperature profile is shown in Figure 7.1.3 below. Differing temperature behaviors lead to the identification of four main layer—the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, and thermosphere—and three isothermal layers called "pauses"—the tropopause, stratopause, and mesopause.
    🔗
    Notice temperature on average cools with increasing height in the troposphere, at \(6.5\text{°C} \, \text{km}^{-1}\) according to the U.S. Standard Atmosphere. The troposphere is the atmospheric layer in which nearly all weather occurs and in which we spend all (or, if we fly, nearly all) of our time. This average temperature behavior in the troposphere motivates the definition of a lapse rate as a rate of temperature decrease with increasing height.

    Subsection 7.1.7 Poisson’s equation and potential temperature

    The DALR (7.1.35) gives the rate at which a perfectly dry air parcel cools with increasing height as it rises adiabatically (or the rate at which it warms with decreasing height as it sinks adiabatically). But it does not give the actual temperature of such an air parcel at any point during its ascent (or descent).
    🔗
    To find the temperature \(T\) a perfectly dry air parcel would have after moving adiabatically from its initial pressure level \(p_0\) with initial temperature \(T_0\) to pressure level \(p\text{,}\) set \(\delta q = 0\) in the related form of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics (7.1.28), rearrange the ideal gas law (7.1.3) to replace \(\alpha\text{,}\) separate, and integrate. This results in Poisson’s equation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{T}{T_0} = \left( \frac{p}{p_0} \right)^\frac{R_d}{c_p}\tag{7.1.36} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Note that [Petty (2008)] and other texts substitute \(\kappa = \dfrac{R_d}{c_p}\) into (7.1.36).
    🔗
    Poisson’s equation (7.1.36) is used to define potential temperature \(\theta [K]\) by replacing \(T_0\) with \(\theta\) and rearranging to solve for \(\theta\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \theta = T \left( \frac{p_0}{p} \right)^\frac{R_d}{c_p}\tag{7.1.37} \end{equation}
    🔗
    We interpret \(\theta\) as the temperature a perfectly dry air parcel would have after moving adiabatically to pressure level \(p_0\) from its current pressure level \(p\) where it has temperature \(T\text{.}\) Thus, for any given value of \(p_0\text{,}\) a perfectly dry air parcel has a unique, conserved value of \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Usually, we set \(p_0 = 1000 \, \text{hPa}\) as a mathematically simple approximation to Earth’s standard mean sea level pressure, although any convenient reference value can be used.

    Subsection 7.1.8 Buoyancy

    Weight is the gravity force experienced by an object due to its mass \(m\) [kg]. The magnitude of weight \(W\) [N] is given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation} W = mg\tag{7.1.38} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(g\) [\(\text{m} \, \text{s}^{-2}\)] is the gravity acceleration.
    🔗
    The definition of the buoyancy force follows from Archimedes’ Principle, which states that the upward force exerted on an object immersed in a fluid by the surrounding fluid equals the magnitude of the weight of the fluid the object has displaced. The magnitude of the weight of the displaced fluid \(W_{fluid}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} W_{fluid} = m_{fluid} \, g\tag{7.1.39} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(m_{fluid}\) is the mass of the displaced fluid. If a volume \(V\) [\(\text{m}^{3}\)] of fluid with constant density \(\rho_{fluid}\) [\(\text{kg} \, \text{m}^{-3}\)] is displaced by an object with volume \(V\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} m_{fluid} = \rho_{fluid} \, V\tag{7.1.40} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and (7.1.39) becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation} W_{fluid} = \rho_{fluid} \, V g\tag{7.1.41} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (7.1.41) gives the upward force the surrounding fluid exerts on the immersed object.
    🔗
    The immersed object also experiences the downward force of gravity as expressed by its own weight \(W_{object}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} W_{object} = -m_{object} \, g\tag{7.1.42} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(m_{object}\) is the mass of the object. As noted above, the object has the same volume \(V\) as the fluid it displaces. If the object has constant density \(\rho_{object}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} m_{object} = \rho_{object} \, V\tag{7.1.43} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and (7.1.42) becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation} W_{object} = -\rho_{object} \, V g\tag{7.1.44} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The buoyancy force \(F_B\) is the sum of these vertically-directed forces acting on the immersed object:
    🔗
    \begin{align} F_B \amp = W_{fluid} + W_{object}\notag\\ \amp = \rho_{fluid} \, V g - \rho_{object} \, V g\notag\\ \amp = (\rho_{fluid} - \rho_{object}) \, V g\tag{7.1.45} \end{align}
    🔗
    It follows from (7.1.45) that if the object is less dense than the fluid it displaces (i.e., \(\rho_{object} \lt \rho_{fluid}\)), then the buoyancy force acting on it is directed upward (i.e., \(F_B \gt 0\)). Likewise, if the object is denser than the fluid it displaces (i.e., \(\rho_{object} \gt \rho_{fluid}\)), then the buoyancy force acting on it is directed downward (i.e., \(F_B \lt 0\)).
    🔗
    In meteorology, we typically take the immersed object to be an air parcel and the fluid within which it is immersed to be the air surrounding it, i.e., its environment. Following the notation of [Martin (2006)], we replace \(\rho_{fluid} = \rho\) for the environmental density and \(\rho_{object} = \rho'\) for the air parcel density. Then (7.1.45) becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation} F_B = (\rho - \rho') \, V g\tag{7.1.46} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Since measuring the masses of air parcels is not feasible, it is convenient to deal with forces per unit mass, i.e., accelerations. It follows that we divide the buoyancy force (7.1.46) by the mass of the air parcel \(m' = \rho' \, V\) to produce the buoyancy acceleration \(a_B\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} a_B = \frac{F_B}{m'} = \frac{(\rho - \rho') \, V g}{\rho' \, V} = \left( \frac{\rho - \rho'}{\rho'} \right) g\tag{7.1.47} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Assuming the air parcel (1) is perfectly dry and (2) has pressure that instantaneously adjusts to the environmental pressure (i.e., \(p' = p\text{,}\) which is a common and accurate assumption under most conditions), we can rewrite the buoyancy acceleration (7.1.47) using the ideal gas law (7.1.2):
    🔗
    \begin{equation} a_B = \left( \frac{T' - T}{T} \right) g\tag{7.1.48} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(T\) is the environmental temperature and \(T'\) is the air parcel temperature. Furthermore, using the definition of potential temperature given in (7.1.37), we can rewrite the buoyancy acceleration (7.1.48) in terms of potential temperature \(\theta\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} a_B = \left( \frac{\theta' - \theta}{\theta} \right) g\tag{7.1.49} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\theta\) is the environmental potential temperature and \(\theta'\) is the air parcel potential temperature.
    🔗
    The alternative forms of the buoyancy acceleration given by (7.1.47)–(7.1.49) lead to the following three cases, which are depicted in Figure 7.1.4 below.
    🔗
    • When a perfectly dry air parcel is denser and therefore colder and potentially colder than its environment (i.e., \(\rho' \gt \rho\text{,}\) \(T' \lt T\text{,}\) and \(\theta' \lt \theta\)), the air parcel is negatively buoyant. In other words, \(F_B\) and \(a_B\) are directed downward (i.e., \(F_B \lt 0\) and \(a_B \lt 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • When a perfectly dry air parcel is less dense and therefore warmer and potentially warmer than its environment (i.e., \(\rho' \lt \rho\text{,}\) \(T' \gt T\text{,}\) and \(\theta' \gt \theta\)), the air parcel is positively buoyant. In other words, \(F_B\) and \(a_B\) are directed upward (i.e., \(F_B \gt 0\) and \(a_B \gt 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • When a perfectly dry air parcel has the same density and therefore the same temperature and potential temperature as its environment (i.e., \(\rho' = \rho\text{,}\) \(T' = T\text{,}\) and \(\theta' = \theta\)), the air parcel is neutrally buoyant. In other words, \(F_B = 0\) and \(a_B = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection 7.1.9 Local static stability

    Differentiating the definition of potential temperature (7.1.37) with respect to geometric height \(z\) produces
    🔗
    \begin{align} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \amp = \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( T \left( \frac{p_0}{p} \right)^\frac{R_d}{c_p} \right)\notag\\ \amp = \left( \frac{p_0}{p} \right)^\frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} + \frac{R_d}{c_p} T \left( \frac{p_0}{p} \right)^{\frac{R_d}{c_p}-1} \left( - \frac{p_0}{p^2} \right) \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}\notag\\ \amp = \left( \frac{p_0}{p} \right)^\frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} - \frac{R_d}{c_p} T \left( \frac{p_0}{p} \right)^\frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{1}{p} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\theta}{T} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} - \frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{\theta}{p} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}\tag{7.1.50} \end{align}
    🔗
    Assuming the atmosphere is in hydrostatic balance, (7.1.50) can be written
    🔗
    \begin{align} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \amp = \frac{\theta}{T} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} - \frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{\theta}{p} (-\rho g)\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\theta}{T} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} + \frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{\theta}{p} \rho g\tag{7.1.51} \end{align}
    🔗
    Substituting the ideal gas law (7.1.2) into (7.1.51) produces
    🔗
    \begin{align} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \amp = \frac{\theta}{T} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} + \frac{R_d}{c_p} \frac{\theta}{p} \left( \frac{p}{R_dT} \right) g\notag\\ \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \amp = \frac{\theta}{T} \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} + \frac{\theta}{T} \frac{g}{c_p}\notag\\ \frac{T}{\theta} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \amp = \frac{\partial T}{\partial z} + \frac{g}{c_p}\tag{7.1.52} \end{align}
    🔗
    Finally, substituting the definitions of the environmental lapse rate (7.1.32) and the DALR (7.1.35) into (7.1.52) produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{T}{\theta} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} = \Gamma_d - \Gamma\tag{7.1.53} \end{equation}
    🔗
    This leads to three cases for local static stability, which according to [Petty (2008)] is the stability of atmospheric layers to small vertical displacements of air parcels within them. The term "local" is used to emphasize that air parcel vertical displacements are small and so only information about the local environmental lapse rate is needed.
    🔗
    • When \(\Gamma_d \gt \Gamma\) (i.e., when a perfectly dry air parcel rising adiabatically cools faster with increasing height than its environment), \(\dfrac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \gt 0\text{.}\) The air parcel is negatively buoyant, and thus \(\dfrac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \gt 0\) is a statement of static stability. In other words, the layer of air surrounding the air parcel is stably stratified. In this type of layer, the air parcel will tend to return to its original location if slightly disturbed vertically.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When \(\Gamma_d \lt \Gamma\) (i.e., when a dry air parcel rising adiabatically cools slower with increasing height than its environment), \(\dfrac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \lt 0\text{.}\) The air parcel is positively buoyant, and thus \(\dfrac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} \lt 0\) is a statement of static instability. In other words, the layer of air surrounding the air parcel is unstably stratified. In this type of layer, the air parcel will convect freely away from its original location if slightly disturbed vertically.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When \(\Gamma_d = \Gamma\) (i.e., when a dry air parcel rising adiabatically cools at the same rate with increasing height as its environment), \(\dfrac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} = 0\text{.}\) The air parcel is neutrally buoyant, and thus \(\dfrac{\partial \theta}{\partial z} = 0\) is a statement of static neutrality. In other words, the layer of air is neutrally stratified. In this type of layer, the air parcel will move to wherever it is displaced and remain there, or it will continue moving with constant speed in the direction of vertical displacement.
      🔗
      🔗
