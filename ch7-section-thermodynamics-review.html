    Section 7.1 Review of dry thermodynamics

    I encourage you to revisit [Petty (2008)] for further discussions of concepts in this chapter, as many concepts are referenced from his text.
    🔗
    Additionally, much of what is discussed in this chapter is review from previous meteorology courses, especially courses that include or are devoted to thermodynamics. We will not discuss all concepts in this chapter in class since many are review. Please read through this chapter fully and, if anything seems unfamiliar or fuzzy, cross-reference with [Petty (2008)] and your previous course notes.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.1 Ideal gases and the ideal gas law

    An ideal gas consists of point particles that move randomly and continuously but do not interact with each other except possibly through perfectly elastic collisions. The condition of non-interaction means ideal gas particles are not attracted to each other and do not undergo phase change.
    🔗
    In theory, any gas can be described by an equation of state that relates its pressure, temperature, and volume. An ideal gas has a simplified equation of state called the ideal gas law. The ideal gas law comes from a combination of Avagadro’s law, Boyle’s law, and Charles’s law and traditionally is written
    🔗
    \begin{equation} pV = nR^*T\tag{7.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(p\) [Pa] is the pressure of the ideal gas, \(V\) [\(\text{m}^3\)] is its volume, \(n\) [mol] is the amount of substance in the gas, \(T\) [K] is the gas’s temperature, and \(R^* = 8.314 \, \text{J} \, \text{K}^{-1} \, \text{mol}^{-1}\) is the universal gas constant.
    🔗
    For many reasons, air is not an ideal gas. For one, its particles have spatial extent. Organized motion often is imposed on them by external forces, resulting in wind. Some of its particles are attracted to each other and to other substances; arguably most importantly for meteorologists, water molecules regularly experience strong intermolecular attractions as well as attractions to surfaces that result in phase change.
    🔗
    Luckily, since air molecules are small and attractions usually are weak, air can be treated as an ideal gas with great accuracy under typical atmospheric conditions. Furthermore, we easily can modify its equations to account for the presence of water vapor, such as by replacing temperature with virtual temperature.
    🔗
    Meteorologists typically express the ideal gas law for air as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p = \rho R T\tag{7.1.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p \alpha = R T\tag{7.1.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\rho\) [\(\text{kg} \text{m}^{-3}\)] is the air density, \(\alpha = \dfrac{1}{\rho}\) [\(\text{m}^{3} \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is its specific volume, and \(R\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\)] is a gas constant appropriate to the particular gas mixture making up the air.
    🔗
    For perfectly dry air (air with no water vapor), we write (7.1.2) and (7.1.3) as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p = \rho R_d T\tag{7.1.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p \alpha = R_d T\tag{7.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    respectively, where \(R_d = 287 \, \text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\) is the gas constant for dry air.
    🔗
    Although as briefly discussed above we can modify the ideal gas law to apply to pure water vapor and humid (moist) air, for simplicity, the following discussions will refer to perfectly dry air. Please refer to your notes from other courses—particularly your thermodynamics course—and [Petty (2008)] for discussions about applying the ideal gas law to pure water vapor and humid air.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.2 Hydrostatic balance

    For an air parcel in hydrostatic balance (also called hydrostatic equilibrium), the upward-directed vertical pressure gradient force balances the downward-directed gravity force (Figure 7.1.1), and other forces can be neglected. Since the air parcel does not accelerate, the vertical equation of motion ((5.4.3) of Chapter 5) reduces to
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} - g\tag{7.1.6} \end{equation}
    🔗
    This balance typically is rearranged
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho g\tag{7.1.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where the negative sign indicates pressure decreases with increasing height in the atmosphere.
    🔗
    When (7.1.7) is separated and integrated, it reveals that the pressure measured at some geometric height is the weight of the atmospheric column above a unit area of measurement at that height.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} - \partial p \amp = \rho g \, \partial z\\ - \int_{p(z)}^{p(\infty)} \partial p \amp = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\\ \int_{p(\infty)}^{p(z)} \partial p \amp = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\\ p(z) - p(\infty) \amp = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since \(p(\infty) = 0\text{,}\) this produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p(z) = \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\tag{7.1.8} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\displaystyle \int_z^{\infty} \rho g \, \partial z\) is the weight of a column of air with horizontal cross-section of unit area above geometric height \(z\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Likewise, separation and integration of (7.1.7) between geometric heights \(z_1\) and \(z_2\text{,}\) where \(z_1 \lt z_2\text{,}\) reveals the difference in pressure measured at two geometric heights is the weight of the atmospheric column with horizontal cross-section of unit area contained between those heights:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} p(z_1) - p(z_2) = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \rho g \, \partial z\tag{7.1.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(p(z_1) > p(z_2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (7.1.7) also could be written
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial z}\tag{7.1.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    or even
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial p} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\tag{7.1.11} \end{equation}
    🔗
    using the definition of geopotential \(\Phi\) presented in Section 4.8.
    🔗
    Note
    🔗
    1. (7.1.7) is diagnostic (descriptive) rather than prognostic (predictive) like the vertical equation of motion because it has no dependence on \(t\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    2. vertical motion \(w\) can no longer be solved for using (7.1.7) since \(w\) does not appear in the equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Hydrostatic balance would apply perfectly to the atmosphere if the air was at rest. When we apply hydrostatic balance to a fluid in motion, we call it the hydrostatic approximation (emphasis on approximation) since it is no longer perfectly accurate. But under most atmospheric conditions, excepting those with very large vertical accelerations, the hydrostatic approximation is accurate. Consequently, we often approximate atmospheric pressure as the weight per unit area of the atmosphere above the height at which pressure is measured.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.3 Hypsometric equation

    We return to hydrostatic balance as expressed by (7.1.7) and rearrange the ideal gas law for perfectly dry air ((7.1.4)) to replace \(\rho = \dfrac{p}{R_dT}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\frac{pg}{R_dT}\tag{7.1.12} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (7.1.12) is a separable equation. We separate its variables
    🔗
    \begin{equation} -\frac{R_dT}{g} \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \partial z\tag{7.1.13} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and integrate it
    🔗
    \begin{equation} - \int_{p_1}^{p_2} \frac{R_dT}{g} \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \, \partial z\tag{7.1.14} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(z_1 \lt z_2\text{.}\) Since pressure decreases with increasing height according to (7.1.7) (and this is observed in nature), \(p_1 \gt p_2\text{,}\) and consequently, it can be helpful to reverse the order of integration on the left-hand side of (7.1.14), which also eliminates the negative sign.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \int_{p_2}^{p_1} \frac{R_dT}{g} \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \, \partial z\tag{7.1.15} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (Note that many texts, including [Martin (2006)] and [Holton and Hakim (2013)], write \(\dfrac{1}{p} \, \partial p\) as \(\partial \ln{p}\text{.}\) This is valid alternative notation.)
    🔗
    If \(R_d\text{,}\) \(T\text{,}\) and \(g\) are held constant, we can integrate (7.1.15) easily. While holding \(R_d\) and \(g\) constant is reasonable in the troposphere since they vary little within it, \(T\) decreases with increasing height linearly on average in the troposphere and cools by over \(60 \, \text{K}\) (as will be discussed in Subsection 7.1.6 below). To avoid writing \(T\) as a linear function of \(z\text{,}\) which would complicate integration, we typically set \(T = \bar{T}\) in (7.1.15), where
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{T} = \frac{1}{z_2 - z_1} \int_{z_1}^{z_2} T \, \partial z \bar{T} = \frac {\int_{p_2}^{p_1} T \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p} {\int_{p_2}^{p_1} \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p}\tag{7.1.16} \end{equation}
    🔗
    is the pressure-weighted column-average temperature. Serendipitously, this assumption does not introduce much error! Then, integrating (7.1.15) produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{R_d \bar{T}}{g} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)} = z_2 - z_1\tag{7.1.17} \end{equation}
    🔗
    By defining geometric height thickness (usually simply called thickness) as the vertical depth between two pressure levels,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta z = z_2 - z_1\tag{7.1.18} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(z_1\) is the geometric height of pressure level \(p_1\) and \(z_2\) is the geometric height of pressure level \(p_2\text{,}\) (7.1.17) produces the hypsometric equation.
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta z = \frac{R_d \bar{T}}{g} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)}\tag{7.1.19} \end{equation}
    🔗
    According to (7.1.19), the warmer the column-average temperature \(\bar{T}\) between pressure levels \(p_1\) and \(p_2\text{,}\) the greater the thickness \(\Delta z\) of the column of air bounded between those levels. Physically, this is because the faster-moving molecules of warmer air travel greater distances in any given amount of time and expand the column of air. (This also lowers the density of warmer air relative to otherwise-identical colder air.)
    🔗
    Returning to (7.1.12), we can separate it somewhat differently:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} -R_dT \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = g \, \partial z\tag{7.1.20} \end{equation}
    🔗
    In this case, the right-hand side is equivalent to \(\partial \Phi\text{.}\) Then we can integrate
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \int_{p_2}^{p_1} R_dT \, \frac{1}{p} \, \partial p = \int_{\Phi_1}^{\Phi_2} \partial \Phi\tag{7.1.21} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (with the order of integration reversed on the left-hand side as before) and apply the pressure-weighted column-average temperature as before to produce
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta \Phi = R_d \bar{T} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)}\tag{7.1.22} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta \Phi = \Phi_2 - \Phi_1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is geopotential thickness, with \(\Phi_1\) as the geopotential at pressure level \(p_1\text{,}\) \(\Phi_2\) as the geopotential at pressure level \(p_2\text{,}\) and \(\Phi_1 \lt \Phi_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Dividing (7.1.22) by \(g_0\) and inserting the definition of geopotential height \(Z = \dfrac{\Phi}{g_0}\) further produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Delta Z = \frac{R_d \bar{T}}{g_0} \ln{\left( \frac{p_1}{p_2} \right)}\tag{7.1.23} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The interpretation of (7.1.23) is essentially the same as the interpretation of (7.1.19): According to (7.1.23), the warmer the column-average temperature \(\bar{T}\) between pressure levels \(p_1\) and \(p_2\text{,}\) the greater the geopotential height thickness
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta Z = Z_2 - Z_1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    of the column of air bounded between those levels. As expected, \(Z_1\) is the geopotential height of pressure level \(p_1\text{,}\) \(Z_2\) is the geopotential height of pressure level \(p_2\text{,}\) and \(Z_1 \lt Z_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Figure 7.1.2 below visualizes how a warmer column of air has greater (geometric height, geopotential, and geopotential height) thickness than a colder column of air bounded by the same pressure levels.

    Subsection 7.1.4 \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics

    An air parcel often is conceptualized as a closed thermodynamic system, which is a thermodynamic system that does not exchange mass with its environment but may exchange energy with it through heat and work.
    🔗
    The \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics is a statement of energy conservation for a closed thermodynamic system. It dictates that heat added to a closed thermodynamic system from its environment through absorption results in an increase in the system’s internal (stored) energy and/or causes the system to do work on its environment. This verbal statement is expressed mathematically as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta q = du + \delta w\tag{7.1.24} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(q\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is heat per unit mass (i.e., specific heat), \(u\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is internal energy per unit mass (i.e, specific internal energy), and \(w\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is mechanical work per unit mass (i.e., specific work). (7.1.24) is the form of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics presented in [Petty (2008)]. Further presentations of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law specific to the atmosphere also are consistent with Petty (2008), and the reader should revisit that text for discussion of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics and related topics.
    🔗
    Note that both the inexact differential \(\delta f\) and the exact differential \(df\) are used in (7.1.24) to represent infinitesimal changes. These notations are used in thermodynamics to remind readers that macroscopic (integral) changes in process variables like \(q\) and \(w\) are path dependent, while macroscopic changes in state variables like \(u\) are path independent, respectively. In the context of the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics, this means \(\Delta u = u_2 - u_1\) but \(\Delta q \neq q_2 - q_1\) and \(\Delta w \neq w_2 - w_1\text{.}\) Revisit [Petty (2008)] for discussion of state and process variables and exact and inexact differentials.
    🔗
    Also note that work is positive (negative) when it is done by (on) the closed thermodynamic system on (by) its environment.
    🔗
    If a gas does only pressure-volume work on its environment,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta w = p \, d\alpha\tag{7.1.25} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\delta w \gt 0\) for expansion and \(\delta w \lt 0\) for contraction. Furthermore, according to Joule’s \(2^{\text{nd}}\) law, the internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on its temperature since its particles are not attracted to each other, and so
    🔗
    \begin{equation} du \propto dT\tag{7.1.26} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Applying these assumptions to (7.1.24) for a perfectly dry air parcel produces
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta q = c_v \, dT + p \, d\alpha\tag{7.1.27} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(c_v = 717 \, \text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\) is the specific heat capacity of dry air at constant volume.
    🔗
    Substitution of the differential of (7.1.3) into (7.1.27) and subsequent rearrangement produce the related form
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \delta q = c_p \, dT - \alpha \, dp\tag{7.1.28} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(c_p = c_v + R_d = 1004 \, \text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1} \, \text{K}^{-1}\) is the specific heat capacity of dry air at constant pressure. \(c_p \gt c_v\) since heat added to an air parcel during an isobaric process causes the air parcel to expand as well as warm, and so less energy is available for warming.
    🔗
    Using the ideal gas law and the definition
    🔗
    \begin{equation} ds = \frac{\delta q}{T}\tag{7.1.29} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(s\) [\(\text{J} \, \text{kg}^{-1}\)] is the entropy per unit mass (i.e., specific entropy) for a reversible thermodynamic process, we can write (7.1.27) and (7.1.28) as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} ds = \frac{c_v}{T} \, dT + \frac{R_d}{\alpha} \, d\alpha\tag{7.1.30} \end{equation}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation} ds = \frac{c_p}{T} \, dT - \frac{R_d}{p} \, dp\tag{7.1.31} \end{equation}
    🔗
    respectively.
    🔗
    As we can see, there are many ways to modify the \(1^{\text{st}}\) law of thermodynamics, and the most useful form can be chosen for a given application.

    Subsection 7.1.5 Lapse rates and adiabatic processes

    A lapse rate is the rate of temperature decrease with increasing height. In other words, it is the rate at which temperature cools with increasing height. By this definition, a lapse rate is positive (negative) when temperature cools (warms) with increasing height.
    🔗
    For a location at geometric height \(z\) within Earth’s atmosphere, the environmental lapse rate is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Gamma = -\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\tag{7.1.32} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (Note: Some sources use \(\gamma\) rather than \(\Gamma\text{.}\))
    🔗
    The environmental lapse rate can be used to characterize special atmospheric layers by their temperature behavior.
    🔗
    • A temperature inversion is a layer of the atmosphere in which temperature warms with increasing height, and so \(\Gamma \lt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • An isothermal layer is a layer of the atmosphere in which temperature is constant with height, and so \(\Gamma = 0 \, \text{K} \, \text{m}^{-1}\) (or any other appropriate unit).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    An adiabatic process is one in which a closed thermodynamic system does not exchange energy with its environment via heat (although it still can exchange energy with its environment via work). Thus, \(\delta q = 0\) for an adiabatic process.
    🔗
    We apply the adiabatic assumption to (7.1.28), divide by \(\delta z\text{,}\) and let \(\delta z \to 0\) to produce
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = c_p \frac{dT}{dz} - \alpha \frac{dp}{dz}\tag{7.1.33} \end{equation}
    🔗
    (The ordinary derivative can be used if we assume the air parcel only moves in the vertical; otherwise, a partial derivative can be used.)
    🔗
    Since \(\alpha = \dfrac{1}{\rho}\text{,}\) the second term in (7.1.33) can be replaced with \(g\) by assuming the air parcel is in hydrostatic balance:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} 0 = c_p \frac{dT}{dz} + g\tag{7.1.34} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Rearranging (7.1.34) to solve for \(-\dfrac{dT}{dz}\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \Gamma_d = -\frac{dT}{dz} = \frac{g}{c_p}\tag{7.1.35} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where \(\Gamma_d\) [\(\text{K} \, \text{m}^{-1}\)] is the dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR). The DALR gives the rate at which a perfectly dry air parcel cools with increasing height as it rises adiabatically while remaining in hydrostatic balance. (Likewise, it gives the rate at which a perfectly dry air parcel warms with decreasing height as it sinks adiabatically while remaining in hydrostatic balance.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.6 Standard atmosphere

    The American Meteorological Society (AMS) defines a standard atmosphere as "a hypothetical vertical distribution of atmospheric temperature, pressure and density that, by international agreement, is taken to be representative of the atmosphere." A standard atmosphere is subject to the assumptions that the air can be treated as an ideal gas and is in hydrostatic balance (at least at lower altitudes). Some standard atmospheres—including the U.S. Standard Atmosphere—are further subject to the assumptions that the air is dry and homogeneously mixed (at least at lower altitudes) and the atmosphere rotates along with Earth, is horizontally homogeneous, is calm, and is a representative average over the diurnal and seasonal cycles.
    🔗
    Several common—and nearly identical—standard atmospheres apply to locations in the middle latitudes, including the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) International Standard Atmosphere (ISA); the U.S. Standard Atmosphere; and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Standard Atmosphere. The U.S. Standard Atmosphere extends through the largest depth (\(z_{min} = -5 \, \text{km}\text{,}\) \(z_{max} = 1000 \, \text{km}\)) and is largely consistent with other commonly used standard atmospheres, and therefore it is chosen for brief presentation here.
    🔗
    The lower portion (through \(z = 130 \, \text{km}\)) of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere’s vertical temperature profile is shown in Figure 7.1.3 below. Differing temperature behaviors lead to the identification of four main layer—the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, and thermosphere—and three isothermal layers called "pauses"—the tropopause, stratopause, and mesopause.
    🔗
    Notice temperature on average cools with increasing height in the troposphere, at \(6.5\text{°C} \, \text{km}^{-1}\) according to the U.S. Standard Atmosphere. The troposphere is the atmospheric layer in which nearly all weather occurs and in which we spend all (or, if we fly, nearly all) of our time. This average temperature behavior in the troposphere motivates the definition of a lapse rate as a rate of temperature decreasewith increasing height.
